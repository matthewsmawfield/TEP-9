6. Physical interpretation and decisive tests

6.1 What conventional alternatives have and have not been excluded

Direct perturber insertions constrain the particular masses, orbits, phases and integration intervals tested (steps 062, 083, 087, 091 and 093). They show that the added bodies produce only small changes to the propagated comet rotations in those configurations, without establishing that a point mass must supply the entire catalogued rotation: most of that rotation is already reproduced by the standard planetary baseline. Mass inversions that require an extra body to generate that whole angle are response-scale exercises, not measurements of a missing mass.

A point mass does not necessarily produce a detectable net orbital-energy difference whenever it deflects a trajectory. A static conservative potential can change direction while preserving asymptotic energy; stationary metric dynamics likewise admits a conserved energy associated with time-translation symmetry. Thus “rotation without detected energy contrast” is not a unique discriminator between gravity and a temporal boundary. Energy-equivalence claims require confidence bounds and an explicit alternative's predicted effect size, rather than a failure to reject a difference.

The same care applies to drag, outgassing and Galactic tides. The direct tide integrations test a specified force tensor over the simulated segments; the latitude-conditioned sky null is a separate population reference and is not a full Galactic-tide population model. Fits without nongravitational terms can absorb omitted cometary recoil into orbital elements. Null correlations with selected covariates narrow explanations without excluding all their interactions or establishing the physical absence of a force.

6.2 A temporal-boundary model must predict observables

A concrete TEP test must supply a field configuration $\phi(\boldsymbol x,t)$, the functions $A$ and $B$, boundary conditions and the environmental response allowed by the canonical theory. It must then predict the matter trajectories and recorded measurements. A spatially varying conformal factor generally changes the matter equations of motion; it cannot simply be inserted as a phase offset while holding every other sector fixed by assumption.

The distinction is especially clear for an integrability claim. For a smooth, single-valued conformal factor, $\Sigma=d\ln A$ is exact and $\oint\Sigma=0$ on a closed loop. Nonzero residual synchronization holonomy requires the additional transport structure specified in the canonical framework. The catalogue tests here do not measure a closed synchronization loop, and a static scalar gradient alone does not demonstrate non-integrable time.

The mathematical bridge to the observations can be stated without inventing a measured lapse. Let $\boldsymbol y$ contain astrometry or clock observables, $\boldsymbol\eta$ the conventional orbit and calibration parameters, and $\boldsymbol\alpha$ a finite set of physical TEP parameters. A model predicts $$\boldsymbol y=\boldsymbol f_{\rm GR}(\boldsymbol\eta)+\boldsymbol s(\boldsymbol\eta,\boldsymbol\alpha)+\boldsymbol\epsilon.$$ For a locally linear conventional fit with design matrix $J$ and inverse noise covariance $W$, the fitted nuisance parameters absorb the component $P\boldsymbol s$, where $$P=J(J^TWJ)^{-1}J^TW,$$ with an appropriate generalized inverse if needed. The surviving signature is $(I-P)\boldsymbol s$. The same projection must be used in an injection-recovery test. This explains why a fitted orbital-element change cannot be identified with an accumulated proper-time offset without deriving the signal $\boldsymbol s$ and its absorption.

A successful model would connect the resident distribution and comet observables with shared parameters, explain the sign and temporal limits of each response, and remain compatible with clock and spacecraft constraints under their actual measurement procedures. The present analysis does not contain that completed derivation; its contribution is to identify the observational patterns and failure modes such a derivation must confront.

6.3 A falsifiable protocol

The next test should freeze the physical and statistical model before inspecting a new sample. The protocol must state the training objects, reference frame, axis, cap or continuous profile, orbital cuts, survey-selection function, observation-quality requirements, integration failures, primary outcome, effect direction and stopping rule. Changing any one of them after examining the validation set creates a new model requiring another validation sample. A temporal split in already available data is useful, but should not be represented as an externally registered future prediction without the corresponding record.

For the resident channel, apply the characterized survey simulator to both an intrinsic isotropic population and the proposed field-shaped population. Include magnitude, motion, sky coverage, tracking efficiency and the secure-orbit cut. Deduplicate objects across catalogues. Fit common parameters on training data and compare predictive likelihoods on the withheld discoveries. If the claimed excess follows survey selection after this calibration, the intrinsic-sector interpretation fails.

For the comet channel, simulate observations from both the conventional and specified TEP trajectories at the actual station epochs, with catalogued uncertainty and plausible correlated astrometric errors. Pass both through the identical orbit fitter and selection rules. Demonstrate recovery of injected signals, coverage of uncertainty intervals, false-positive calibration and convergence with integration tolerances and boundary extraction. The primary comparison must be at the observation level or a derived statistic with its validated covariance, not a reduced regression on a quantity generated by standard dynamics.

The modern-catalogue control should be part of this protocol, with composition-by-sky and station-by-epoch interactions tested or marginalized. A revised moving or bipolar boundary must predict its lobe locations and transition times from training data; merely fitting the observed reversal is insufficient. If the frozen prediction fails again, that model should be rejected without moving the target.

6.4 Forecasts are conditional

The resident cap fraction of roughly 0.59 and the adopted footprint baseline of roughly 0.42 motivate a prospective sample-size calculation (steps 054 and 067). They are measured or fitted sample fractions under the current selection assumptions, not a parameter-free TEP prediction for another survey. A future survey's expected fraction and power must be recalculated using its own selection function and uncertainty in the training estimates. No calendar date or discovery yield makes the test decisive before that calibration.

These requirements strengthen the testability of TEP: they identify a finite set of observations that could support or falsify a specific field model. They also retain the persistent spatial result as a legitimate target for conventional population models. The same quantitative standard should apply to both.
