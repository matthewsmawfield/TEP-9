1. Introduction

1.1 From the established programme to the outer Solar System

This paper develops an outer-Solar-System consequence of the TEP research programme. The canonical matter coupling is supplied by Paper 0; the empirical foundation is supplied by the multi-centre GNSS, long-baseline CODE and raw-RINEX studies (Papers 1, 2 and 3). Paper 33 advances that foundation into predictive clock-network applications, including a phase-resolved prior frozen before its held-out evaluation. TEP-9 takes the field and universal matter coupling as premises of this programme. Its task is to establish the field configuration and observable response in the outer Solar System.

The apparent concentration of some extreme trans-Neptunian perihelia has motivated distant-planet models (Trujillo & Sheppard 2014; Batygin & Brown 2016). Its interpretation remains sensitive to discovery selection. Characterized-survey analyses have found that their extreme-object samples are consistent with an underlying unclustered distribution once selection is included (Shankman et al. 2017; Napier et al. 2021). The application-specific question is whether these populations resolve a common temporal domain after the discovery and measurement processes have been modelled. Resident objects and comets passing through the outer system probe different aspects of that configuration.

A second population offers a useful test because it has different trajectories, discovery histories and fitted observables. Agreement can expose a common organizing direction. It does not by itself identify the organizing mechanism: shared sky visibility, inherited astrometry and analytic choices can also correlate the evidence. Three levels of inference are therefore distinguished throughout: a feature of the observed catalogue; rejection of a specified statistical null; and preference for a physical TEP model over conventional alternatives.

1.2 Imported results and the boundary inference

The companion results enter with distinct roles. Papers 1–3 supply the distance-structured clock field and its persistence across processing approaches; Paper 33 supplies predictive use of its phase structure. Paper 8 contributes optical-domain consistency, Paper 14 a multi-GNSS product stress test, and Paper 17 a compactness-dependent lunar residual channel. Paper 34 gives a particularly relevant observation-model example: a predicted conformal pulsar signal can be largely absorbed by an orbital fit. These are the foundation and methodological constraints for the present extension; their original datasets are not added again as independent TEP-9 detections.

TEP-9 must determine which outer-system direction, radial profile, environmental response and temporal behaviour follow from that foundation. The terrestrial covariance length is a clock-network response scale; it does not itself set a heliospheric radius. Likewise, a non-identifiable three-dimensional direction in MGEX does not supply a sharply localized external axis. The inherited field premise and the new boundary parameters therefore have separate evidential roles.

The canonical TEP framework couples matter to a metric $$\tilde g_{\mu\nu}=A^2(\phi)g_{\mu\nu}+B(\phi)\nabla_\mu\phi\nabla_\nu\phi,$$ where $\phi$ is a scalar field and $A$ and $B$ specify conformal and disformal responses. A localized outer-system transition in this field is the hypothesis under investigation. The boundary should be understood as a continuous field configuration; its position, width, amplitude, motion and environmental response must ultimately follow from a specified model. The statistical cap used here does not supply that solution.

In the conformal limit, along a specified timelike worldline, $d\tilde\tau=A(\phi)\,d\tau_g$. This relation explains why a spatially varying field could affect clock comparisons, but it does not equate an orbital-element angle with elapsed proper time. Matter trajectories, photon propagation, local reference clocks and the orbit-fitting procedure must be treated together. The pulsar absorption analysis in Paper 34 shows why this transfer through the fitted observation model matters even when the underlying field is taken as given.

The cross-domain structure of TEP supplies the physical motivation: one field links orbital and clock responses. A convincing outer-system demonstration must preserve that link through shared parameters. Its sign, axis, epoch dependence and screening response must be derived or estimated on identified training data. The present catalogue results constrain those boundary properties; the theoretical response and observation-model requirements are developed in Section 6.

1.3 Resident and transit probes

The resident sample uses $a>150$ AU and $q>30$ AU; the more extreme subset uses $a>250$ AU. These are operational catalogue cuts. Objects close to the perihelion threshold can still interact with Neptune, so residence in a proposed field region is not established simply by classification. Long-period comets provide a different probe through their angular elements, published original and future orbits, and independently fitted astrometric arcs.

The detached sample yields a direction near $(49.9^\circ,-17.0^\circ)$ in ecliptic J2000 coordinates; the more extreme subset yields approximately $(34^\circ,-13^\circ)$. The latter defines the principal comet cap. These are distinct, correlated estimates from nested resident samples. The analysis records both rather than treating every test as if it used one identical axis. “Resident-derived” denotes their construction; it is not a claim of an externally timestamped preregistration.

Sections 2–4 describe the data and compare the resident, transit and spatial channels. Section 5 addresses combined evidence and spacecraft controls. Section 6 states the physical and statistical tests required to turn the spatial hypothesis into a discriminating TEP prediction.
